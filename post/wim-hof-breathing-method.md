Wim Hof — nicknamed "The Iceman" — first attracted scientific curiosity in 2011 when he was brought into a laboratory and infected with a bacterial endotoxin under controlled conditions. Unlike ordinary people, he was able to suppress his immune response on demand. Researchers were baffled. The experiment has since been replicated, published in peer-reviewed journals, and has helped launch one of the most studied breathing practices in modern wellness science.

But how much of what is claimed about the Wim Hof Method is actually supported by evidence? And how do you practise it safely?

## What Is the Wim Hof Method?

The Wim Hof Method has three components: **breathing exercises**, **cold exposure**, and **meditation/focus**. This article focuses on the breathing component — what Hof himself calls the core of the method.

The breathing technique involves three rounds of:

1. **30 deep, rapid breaths** — fast, full inhales followed by passive, relaxed exhales
2. **Retention hold** — after the final exhale, hold your breath for as long as comfortable (typically 1–3 minutes for beginners)
3. **Recovery breath** — inhale deeply, hold for 15 seconds, then exhale

Each round is followed by the next. Most practitioners complete three rounds per session, which takes roughly 15–20 minutes.

## The Physiology of What Happens

The rapid 30 breaths phase is, in technical terms, a **controlled hyperventilation**. This sounds alarming, but it produces specific, predictable physiological changes:

**CO2 drops sharply.** With each rapid breath, you exhale more CO2 than usual. Blood CO2 levels fall, causing blood pH to rise (respiratory alkalosis). This produces a tingling sensation in the hands, feet, and face — a common and expected sign you are doing the technique correctly.

**The spleen contracts.** Research published in PLOS ONE found that the Wim Hof breathing method causes the spleen to contract and release a surge of red blood cells into circulation, measurably increasing blood oxygen-carrying capacity. This is one reason practitioners report a feeling of energised alertness after a session.

**Adrenaline surges.** The 2014 Radboud University study (the bacterial endotoxin experiment) found that Wim Hof practitioners showed significantly elevated adrenaline levels — comparable to those seen in first-time bungee jumpers — during the breathing exercise. This adrenaline surge is thought to be the mechanism behind the immune-modulating effects.

**The breath hold becomes dramatically extended.** After expelling CO2, your body's urge to breathe is primarily driven by CO2 accumulation — not by oxygen depletion. With CO2 already very low, the retention hold can be sustained for far longer than normal without discomfort. Most beginners can hold for 1.5–2 minutes on their very first attempt.

## What the Research Shows

**Immune modulation.** The 2014 Radboud University study, published in *PNAS*, remains the most cited piece of evidence. Subjects trained in the Wim Hof Method were able to voluntarily suppress their inflammatory response to an injected bacterial endotoxin. The control group experienced typical flu-like symptoms; the trained group had significantly fewer. This was genuinely novel — previously, the autonomic nervous system was considered entirely involuntary.

**Stress resilience.** Multiple studies have measured cortisol levels and self-reported stress in Wim Hof practitioners, consistently finding reduced stress reactivity after regular practice.

**Pain tolerance.** Research has found increased pain thresholds in practitioners, likely mediated by the adrenaline response and endorphin release during the breathing phase.

**Limitations to note.** Most studies have small sample sizes. The original 2014 study included just 24 participants. Long-term effects of daily practice are not yet well characterised. The method should not be seen as a replacement for medical treatment, and the cold exposure component carries its own risks.

## How to Practise the Breathing Technique

**Never practise in or near water.** Loss of consciousness during the retention hold is possible, particularly in cold water. This is a genuine risk — people have drowned practising Wim Hof breathing in the bath. Always practise lying down on dry land.

**Do not practise while driving or operating machinery.**

Once you are in a safe, comfortable position lying down:

1. **Round 1 — 30 power breaths.** Breathe in fully through the nose or mouth, expanding your belly and chest. Then let the breath go — do not forcefully exhale, just release. Do this 30 times in a rhythmic, slightly faster than normal pace. By breath 20–25 you will likely feel tingling and lightheadedness. This is normal.

2. **Retention hold.** After the 30th breath, exhale completely and stop. Do not breathe. Hold for as long as feels comfortable — anywhere from 30 seconds to 3 minutes. Start modest. When the urge to breathe returns, you have reached your hold.

3. **Recovery breath.** Take one large inhale through the nose, filling to maximum capacity. Hold for 15 seconds, then exhale. This completes round one.

4. **Repeat for 3 rounds.** The subsequent rounds typically produce longer retention holds as CO2 levels drop further with each cycle.

## What to Expect in Your First Session

In your first session you will likely experience:

- Tingling in hands, feet, face, and lips
- Light-headedness or a "floaty" feeling
- Spontaneous muscle contractions (carpopedal spasm) — harmless and temporary
- A surprising length on your first retention hold
- A wave of energy and clarity in the 30–60 minutes after the session

Some people feel emotional during or after the practice. The combination of physiological shift and deliberate attention to the body can surface strong feelings. This is considered normal by practitioners and passes quickly.

## Who Should Avoid This Technique

The Wim Hof breathing method is not appropriate for everyone. Avoid it if you:

- Are pregnant
- Have epilepsy or a history of seizures
- Have a serious cardiovascular condition
- Have a history of fainting
- Are currently experiencing a mental health crisis

If you are unsure, consult a doctor before starting.

## A Final Note on Cold Exposure

The breathing practice is one part of the method. Hof himself considers the cold exposure equally important — most commonly taken as cold showers of 1–3 minutes duration. The evidence for cold exposure is separate and growing, covering everything from reduced inflammation and improved mood to metabolic benefits. If the breathing resonates with you, the cold showers are worth exploring.
